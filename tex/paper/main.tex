\begin{abstract}
Hierarchical federated learning reduces direct cloud communication, but fixed
workflows cannot adapt computation, aggregation, and privacy protection to
heterogeneous devices and changing system conditions. We propose a unified
cloud-edge-end collaboration reconfiguration framework that jointly determines
execution placement, aggregation topology, and privacy mechanisms under dynamic
resource and privacy constraints. Link blocks and link flows provide a unified
collaboration space in which heterogeneous federated learning workflows become
comparable decisions. A joint model evaluates latency, communication, privacy
consumption, encrypted aggregation overhead, and estimated convergence error
cost. The resulting multiobjective problem is solved through bounded Pareto
search and an ideal point Tchebycheff criterion. Experiments on CIFAR 10 with
ResNet 18 and Fashion MNIST with LeNet 5 examine privacy feasibility, test
accuracy, communication, and system time including the measured decision cost.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Federated learning (FL) enables multiple end devices to collaboratively train a model by keeping data local and uploading model parameters and gradient based updates~\cite{fedavg}.
With the development of artificial intelligence for IoT (AIoT), mobile edge computing, and intelligent sensing systems, training data are increasingly generated by cameras, mobile terminals, vehicles, and industrial sensors.
Cloud-edge-end hierarchical federated learning (HFL) has therefore been introduced to reduce long distance communication between end devices and the cloud through edge aggregation~\cite{hfl}.
It also enables multiple edge domains to participate in a shared cloud level training process.

However, HFL in cloud-edge-end environments still faces two major challenges.
First, end device data are usually non-IID, and significant distribution differences among devices can lead to model drift and performance degradation under simple weighted aggregation~\cite{fedavgm,fedprox}.
Second, IoT devices are system heterogeneous.
Low power cameras and sensors may be unable to support full training of a large deep model, whereas edge servers and cloud servers usually have stronger computation and memory resources~\cite{wang2019adaptivefl}.
If all devices are forced to follow the same training process, weak and delayed devices may become stragglers and may even be excluded by resource constraints.

Existing work has addressed these issues from several directions, but most studies improve a fixed HFL procedure and leave dynamic assignment of training tasks across the end, edge, and cloud layers insufficiently explored.
Personalized HFL mitigates non-IID drift through edge side personalization, similarity aware aggregation, and model decoupling~\cite{Personalizedhfl,JSJX202502007}.
Resource constrained and wireless FL optimize local update frequency, communication rounds, energy consumption, and wireless resource allocation~\cite{wang2019adaptivefl,dinh2021wirelessfl}.
Cloud-edge-end FL studies further consider heterogeneous model collaboration, multiple server edge aggregation, and split training mechanisms~\cite{BSBODP,mazloomi2025multiserver,SFETEC}.
Nevertheless, most existing methods are built around a specific collaborative procedure with a fixed hierarchical structure.
They pay insufficient attention to the complete collaboration decision, namely which model blocks should be executed at the end, edge, and cloud layers, which aggregation flow should be used, and how this task assignment should interact with privacy mechanisms.

Privacy protection further complicates this decision.
DP limits the influence of one client by clipping and perturbing model updates, but it may degrade convergence.
Adaptive DP methods tune privacy budgets, clipping thresholds, and client selection to improve the privacy utility tradeoff~\cite{GLDP,xu2025adpfl,elabd2025dynamicdp}.
HE, secure aggregation, and privacy preserving offloading have also been studied in FL and cloud-edge-end systems~\cite{dong2026privacyoffloading,PPHFL,islam2025pphffl}.
Different collaborative modes expose model updates at different trust boundaries.
For example, split training keeps intermediate features and backward gradients inside the associated end and edge execution domain, whereas cloud aggregation requires protected model updates to cross that domain boundary.
Therefore, the communication, computation, and estimated convergence error
costs of privacy mechanisms should be modeled together with collaborative mode
selection.

The central difficulty is that privacy protection, training utility, and system efficiency are tightly coupled.
Stronger privacy protection can increase computation and communication overhead and can introduce clipping distortion and perturbation noise.
Moving computation among the end, edge, and cloud layers can relieve resource limited devices, but it also changes cross layer transmission, synchronization latency, and the objects exposed to privacy mechanisms.
Consequently, improving one aspect may degrade another, and a global cloud-edge-end decision must balance privacy protection, training efficiency, and convergence behavior.
Figure~\ref{fig:balance} illustrates this coupled design objective.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\columnwidth]{balance.png}
\caption{Coupled tradeoff among privacy protection, training utility, and efficiency.}
\label{fig:balance}
\end{figure}

Motivated by this challenge, this paper studies dynamic reconfiguration of
cloud-edge-end federated learning workflows under resource and privacy
constraints. Each configuration jointly specifies model execution, computation
placement, aggregation flow, and privacy mechanisms across the end, edge, and
cloud layers.
The main contributions are summarized as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose a dynamic cloud-edge-end collaboration reconfiguration framework that adapts execution placement, aggregation topology, and privacy mechanisms to resource availability and privacy requirements.
    \item We introduce a unified collaboration space representation based on link blocks and link flows. It describes model execution, transmitted objects, aggregation locations, return paths, and privacy mechanisms in a common decision space.
    \item We establish a joint model for latency, communication, privacy consumption, encrypted aggregation overhead, and estimated convergence error cost. Privacy mechanisms are treated as collaboration dimensions instead of external additions to a fixed workflow.
    \item We formulate collaboration reconfiguration as a constrained multiobjective problem. A bounded Pareto search constructs feasible global decisions, and an ideal point Tchebycheff criterion selects the executed configuration.
\end{itemize}
The framework is evaluated through 100 round CIFAR 10 training with ResNet 18
under heterogeneous devices and edge level label skew.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}
\subsection{HFL Under Heterogeneous Environments}
HFL introduces edge servers between end devices and the cloud to reduce direct cloud communication pressure through edge pre aggregation~\cite{hfl}.
Resource aware FL studies reduce stragglers and communication overhead by adjusting client selection, local update frequency, pruning, and model compression under limited computation, energy, and bandwidth resources~\cite{phfl,xu2022adaptive,wang2019adaptivefl,dinh2021wirelessfl}.
Personalized HFL further mitigates performance degradation under non-IID data through edge side personalized models, similarity aggregation, and model decoupling~\cite{Personalizedhfl,JSJX202502007}.
These methods show that HFL can improve training from resource scheduling, communication control, and data heterogeneity adaptation.
However, when end device resources differ across computation, memory, and communication capabilities, using the same training flow for all clients can still cause straggler blocking in conventional synchronous aggregation and prevent weak devices from participating effectively.
This paper jointly considers end device computation constraints, memory constraints, communication overhead, and model split positions during collaboration reconfiguration.
Dynamic selection itself may also introduce reconfiguration overhead. Recent multi job FL studies explicitly model switching cost in client selection and reduce it through block wise selection, where the selected client subset is kept unchanged across multiple rounds~\cite{CHEN2026112204,10757319}. This observation motivates the use of a strategy update period in our framework. Collaborative modes are re evaluated only at update points, which limits unnecessary mode reconfiguration while preserving adaptability to resource and link changes.

\subsection{Cloud-Edge-End Collaborative Federated Learning}
HFL is a representative form of cloud-edge-end collaborative FL, in which end devices, edge nodes, and the cloud jointly execute computation, aggregation, and communication tasks.
As model size and system heterogeneity increase, fixed full end device training followed by edge aggregation becomes less suitable for resource limited IoT devices.
FedAgg~\cite{BSBODP} uses bridge samples and online distillation to enable models of different scales at the end, edge, and cloud layers to collaborate.
SFETEC~\cite{SFETEC} introduces split federated learning into Thing Edge Cloud environments and partitions model computation across layers to reduce end device computation load.
Cloud-edge-end task offloading studies also combine FL, privacy protection, and task efficiency~\cite{dong2026privacyoffloading}.
These studies show the value of collaboration among the three layers, but they usually assume a fixed collaboration structure.
In contrast, this paper studies how each device should select a collaborative mode under given resource and privacy conditions.
The need to balance reconfiguration gain and reconfiguration cost is also consistent with edge computing task migration studies. In mobile edge computing, migration can reduce service delay when users move away from the original edge server, but it also consumes backhaul transmission and reconfiguration resources~\cite{ZHANG2019111}. Load aware task migration further models migration cost as a combination of communication, queuing, and computation costs, and notes that migration cost can be related to physical distance between edge nodes~\cite{ZHU2024303}. These studies suggest that dynamic cloud-edge-end collaboration should account for both the benefit of changing execution paths and the overhead caused by such changes.

\subsection{Privacy Preserving HFL}
Model parameters, gradients, and intermediate representations in federated training may leak information about private data.
Differential privacy (DP), homomorphic encryption (HE), and secure aggregation are therefore widely used in FL.
For HFL, group local DP provides personalized privacy through sampling, randomized response, and shuffling~\cite{GLDP}.
Adaptive DP FL improves convergence and model utility through dynamic privacy budget allocation, adaptive clipping, and client selection~\cite{xu2025adpfl}.
Other privacy preserving HFL studies combine client level DP, secure aggregation, and CKKS style HE for data space scenarios~\cite{PPHFL}. Related fog FL studies combine hierarchical aggregation, personalization, and DP for IoT intrusion detection~\cite{islam2025pphffl}.
These studies mainly focus on the security guarantee, budget allocation, and accuracy degradation of a particular privacy mechanism.
The joint modeling of privacy induced communication cost, computation latency,
estimated convergence error cost, and cloud-edge-end collaboration reconfiguration remains
insufficiently studied.

\section{Model Construction}
\label{sec:model}
\subsection{Cloud-Edge-End Collaborative Link Modeling}
\label{sec:link_model}
We consider a three tier cloud-edge-end collaborative FL system composed of end devices, edge nodes, and a cloud node.
Let \(\mathcal{N}\) denote the set of end devices.
Each end device \(i\in\mathcal{N}\) selects one collaborative mode \(h_i\) from a candidate mode set \(\mathcal{H}\).
In this paper, a collaborative mode specifies the model split position, the layer that executes each model block, the aggregation flow, and the return path of aggregation results across the end, edge, and cloud layers.
Given a global model \(M\), different modes induce the partition
\(M=M_L^{h_i}\cup M_E^{h_i}\cup M_C^{h_i}\), where \(M_L^{h_i}\),
\(M_E^{h_i}\), and \(M_C^{h_i}\) are the submodels executed at the end,
edge, and cloud layers, respectively.
Weaker devices use split positions closer to the input layer and offload more computation toward upper layers, whereas stronger devices can undertake a larger proportion of local training.

Each end device and its associated edge node form a trusted execution domain.
Intermediate representations, logits, backward gradients, and model updates exchanged inside this domain are not exposed to the cloud adversary.
The cloud is honest but curious, and update transmissions from an end device or edge node to the cloud cross the trust boundary.
These cross domain updates are protected by DP or HE.
Raw samples remain at the end device throughout training.

\begin{definition}[Collaborative Link Block]
Given a collaborative mode \(h_i\), a collaborative link block is the local
training step and cross layer interaction associated with end device \(i\)
before an aggregation event. Formally,
\(B_i^{h_i}=(d_i,\ell_i,g_i,\mathcal{E}_{B,i}^{h_i})\), where \(d_i\) is the
participating end device, \(\ell_i\in\{L,E,C\}\) indicates the
update submission layer from which the local model update is uploaded to the
aggregation node before aggregation, \(g_i\in\{A_e,A_c\}\) is the aggregation
node that receives the update, and \(\mathcal{E}_{B,i}^{h_i}\) is the set of
intermediate feature, backward gradient, and model update transmission links
caused by split training.
According to \(\ell_i\), \(B_i^{h_i}\) is classified as \(B_L\), \(B_E\), or \(B_C\), corresponding to a local update submitted for aggregation from the end, edge, or cloud layer, respectively.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Collaborative Link Flow]
A collaborative link flow is a complete training link formed by at least one collaborative link block, the aggregation node, and the model return process.
A one level aggregation collaborative link flow can be abstracted as
\begin{equation}
    \text{Link flow}\quad d_i \xrightarrow{B_i^{h_i}\times L_B} \ell_i
    \xrightarrow{\text{upload}} a
    \xrightarrow{\text{download}} q_i .
\end{equation}
Here, \(\xrightarrow{B_i^{h_i}\times L_B}\) denotes that end device \(i\) performs \(L_B\) link blocks under collaborative mode \(h_i\), with the local update submitted for aggregation from layer \(\ell_i\). \(a\) is the selected aggregation node, and \(q_i\) denotes the specific return receiver for device \(i\). In the modes studied here, \(q_i\) is the end device \(d_i\) when the returned state is used locally, and it is the associated edge node when the returned submodel is kept at the edge side.
If two level aggregation exists, the collaborative link flow also contains an edge aggregation node \(A_e\), the cloud aggregation node \(A_c\), and the final return to the end device.
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \text{Link flow}\quad [d_i \xrightarrow{B_i^{h_i}\times L_B} \ell_i
    \xrightarrow{\text{upload}} A_e] \times E_B\\
    \xrightarrow{\text{upload}} A_c
    \xrightarrow{\text{download}} A_e
    \xrightarrow{\text{download}} d_i .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\(E_B\) denotes the number of edge level aggregation loops completed at \(A_e\) before the edge aggregated update is uploaded to \(A_c\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Cloud-Edge-End Collaborative Link Graph]
For a collaborative mode \(h_i\), its training process is represented by the
directed graph \(\mathcal{G}^{h_i}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})\), where
\(\mathcal{V}\) contains end, edge, and cloud nodes and \(\mathcal{E}\)
contains their interaction links. Each link is denoted by
\(\lambda=(u,v,b)\), where \(u\) and \(v\) are the source and target nodes and
\(b\) is the interaction type.
The link size, rate, and latency are treated as link attributes in the cost model.
\end{definition}

Based on the abstract training flow illustrated in \figref{fig:classic}, the unified representation describes seven basic modes over the end (\(L\)), edge (\(E\)), and cloud (\(C\)) layers. The interfaces mark candidate interaction boundaries from shallow to deep stages. A deeper boundary keeps more computation on the end side, whereas an earlier boundary offloads more computation toward upper layers.
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{base}}=\{\mathrm{LIE},\mathrm{LIC},\mathrm{LIIE},\mathrm{LIIC},\mathrm{LIEIIC},\mathrm{LIEIIIC},\mathrm{LIIEIIIC}\}.
\end{equation}
LIC sends intermediate representations directly to the cloud and is therefore
excluded under the stated trust model. The executable mode set is
\(\mathcal H=\mathcal H_{\mathrm{base}}\setminus\{\mathrm{LIC}\}\).
The symbols \(L\), \(E\), and \(C\) indicate the update submission layer of a collaborative link block, while \(\mathrm{I}\), \(\mathrm{II}\), and \(\mathrm{III}\) denote the collaborative interaction interfaces defined in \figref{fig:classic}.

\begin{definition}[Collaborative Link Transmission Indicator Matrix]
Let the node order be \((L,E,C)\). The transmission types are
\(\mathcal{B}=\{\mathrm{emb},\mathrm{grad},\mathrm{upd}\}\). Here,
\(\mathrm{emb}\) denotes intermediate embedding transmission,
\(\mathrm{grad}\) denotes backward gradient transmission in split training,
and \(\mathrm{upd}\) denotes local, edge aggregated, or global aggregated model
update transmission.
For mode \(h_i\) and transmission type \(b\in\mathcal{B}\), the collaborative link transmission indicator matrix is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{I}_{b}^{h_i}(u,v)=
    \begin{cases}
    1, & \text{mode } h_i \text{ transmits type } b \text{ from } u \text{ to } v,\\
    0, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
For example, for one complete LIEIIC link flow,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{emb}}^{\mathrm{LIEIIC}}=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{grad}}^{\mathrm{LIEIIC}}=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 0 & 0\\
1 & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix},\\
\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{upd}}^{\mathrm{LIEIIC}}=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 0 & 0\\
1 & 0 & 1\\
0 & 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The nonzero entries indicate transmission directions only.
The corresponding candidate mechanism matrix is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{O}_{b}^{h_i}(u,v)=
\begin{cases}
\{\mathrm{trusted}\}, & \mathbf{I}_{b}^{h_i}(u,v)=1,\ (u,v)\in\{(L,E),(E,L)\},\\
\{\mathrm{DP},\mathrm{HE}\}, & \mathbf{I}_{b}^{h_i}(u,v)=1,\ b=\mathrm{upd},\ v=C,\\
\{\mathrm{none}\}, & \mathbf{I}_{b}^{h_i}(u,v)=1,\ \text{model return},\\
\emptyset, & \mathbf{I}_{b}^{h_i}(u,v)=0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Thus, \(\mathbf{I}_{b}^{h_i}\) says whether a transmission exists.
\(\mathcal{O}_{b}^{h_i}\) gives the candidate privacy mechanisms for that transmission.
For LIEIIC, the update mechanism matrix is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{upd}}^{\mathrm{LIEIIC}}=
\begin{pmatrix}
\emptyset & \emptyset & \emptyset\\
\{\mathrm{none}\} & \emptyset & \{\mathrm{DP},\mathrm{HE}\}\\
\emptyset & \{\mathrm{none}\} & \emptyset
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The entries with \(\{\mathrm{none}\}\) are model return links.
\end{definition}

The selected mode determines the transmitted objects, and the indicator matrices record their directions.
Privacy mechanisms are then selected for the eligible transmitted objects.
Embedding and gradient exchanges remain inside the trusted end and edge domain, while updates sent to the cloud may use DP or HE~\cite{PPHFL}.
\(\Pi(h_i)\) denotes the candidate privacy policy set under mode \(h_i\), and each policy \(\pi_i\in\Pi(h_i)\) maps an eligible link to a selected privacy mechanism.
For each transmitted link \(\lambda=(u,v,b)\), the selected privacy mechanism \(\pi_i(\lambda)\) is chosen from \(\mathcal{O}_{b}^{h_i}(u,v)\).
Model return links are counted for communication latency only and are not assigned DP or HE.

\textbf{Collaborative link flows of the seven modes.}
The following expressions summarize the block execution layer, aggregation node, and model return path of each mode.
Detailed transmitted objects and candidate privacy mechanisms are determined by \(\mathbf{I}_{b}^{h_i}\) and \(\mathcal{O}_{b}^{h_i}\).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{LIE} \(\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{LIE}) = B_E \times L_B \rightarrow A_e \rightarrow \mathrm{end}\).
    \item \textbf{LIIE} \(\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{LIIE}) = B_L \times L_B \rightarrow A_e \rightarrow \mathrm{end}\).
    \item \textbf{LIC} \(\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{LIC}) = B_C \times L_B \rightarrow A_c \rightarrow \mathrm{end}\).
    \item \textbf{LIIC} \(\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{LIIC}) = B_L \times L_B \rightarrow A_c \rightarrow \mathrm{end}\).
    \item \textbf{LIEIIC} \(\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{LIEIIC}) = B_E \times L_B \rightarrow A_c \rightarrow A_e \rightarrow \mathrm{end}\).
    \item \textbf{LIEIIIC} \(\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{LIEIIIC}) = [B_E \times L_B \rightarrow A_e \rightarrow \mathrm{end}] \times E_B \rightarrow A_c \rightarrow A_e \rightarrow \mathrm{end}\).
    \item \textbf{LIIEIIIC} \(\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{LIIEIIIC}) = [B_L \times L_B \rightarrow A_e \rightarrow \mathrm{end}] \times E_B \rightarrow A_c \rightarrow A_e \rightarrow \mathrm{end}\).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Joint Cost and Utility Modeling}
\label{sec:cost_utility_model}
After the link topology is determined, each client selects a collaborative mode \(h_i\) and a privacy policy \(\pi_i\).
The selected mode must satisfy the computation and memory limits of the end device, while the joint decision profile determines the system level latency and estimated convergence error cost.
The modeling below is organized in five parts, including end side resource feasibility, path level latency, buffered aggregation cost, DP budget consumption, and convergence error cost estimation.

\subsubsection{End Side Resource Feasibility}
Let \(n_i\) be the number of local samples processed by device \(i\) in one round, and let \(L_B\) be the number of local link block iterations.
Under mode \(h_i\), \(\chi(h_i)\) denotes the profiled computation workload required by the end layer to process one sample.
The subscript \(L\) denotes the local end device layer.
The single round local computation workload is
\(W_{i,L}^{\mathrm{comp}}(h_i)=L_Bn_i\chi(h_i)\).

The value of \(\chi(h_i)\) depends on the model blocks assigned to the end device and is obtained through offline profiling.
When detailed operator level profiling is unavailable, it is approximated as
\(\chi(h_i)\approx\kappa_cm_i(h_i)\), where \(m_i(h_i)\) is the parameter
scale assigned to device \(i\), and \(\kappa_c\) is a calibrated conversion
coefficient.
This provides a lightweight complexity estimate for candidate mode screening and is in line with resource aware FL latency models~\cite{zhou2023joint}.

Let \(F_i\) denote the effective computing rate of device \(i\). The end side
computation latency is
\(T_{i,L}^{\mathrm{comp}}(h_i)=W_{i,L}^{\mathrm{comp}}(h_i)/F_i\).
This follows the common wireless FL latency model, where local computation time depends on local iterations, local data size, per sample computation workload, and device computing rate.
Given the maximum allowable computation time \(\tau_i^{\max}\), the
computation budget is \(Q_{\mathrm{comp},i}=F_i\tau_i^{\max}\).

The memory requirement under mode \(h_i\) is
\(M_i^{\mathrm{mem}}(h_i)=s_{\mathrm{state}}m_i(h_i)+a_i(h_i)\).
This model follows the common separation between parameter related model states and residual training memory~\cite{3433701.3433727}.
Here, \(s_{\mathrm{state}}\) is the profiled per parameter model state memory coefficient, covering parameters, gradients, optimizer states such as Adam momentum and variance, and other persistent training states such as mixed precision parameter copies.
The term \(a_i(h_i)\) denotes residual memory that is not captured by the parameter scale, including activations and temporary training buffers.

Let \(Q_{\mathrm{mem},i}\) denote the available memory of device \(i\).
The resource feasible mode set is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_i^{\mathrm{res}}=
    \{h_i\in\mathcal{H}\mid
    W_{i,L}^{\mathrm{comp}}(h_i)\le Q_{\mathrm{comp},i},
    M_i^{\mathrm{mem}}(h_i)\le Q_{\mathrm{mem},i}\}.
\label{eq:resource_feasible}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Path Level Latency}
We first define the general link and path latency terms. A representative complete path is then illustrated in \figref{fig:time}.
Let \(R^t=(R_{u,v}^t)_{u,v\in\mathcal{V}}\) be the cloud-edge-end link rate matrix in round \(t\).
The communication model is link specific. For an end edge pair \((i,j)\), the rate \(R_{i,j}^{t}\) and the base latency \(T_{i,j}^{0}\) may differ from those of other device edge pairs. These parameters capture heterogeneous physical distance, wireless channel quality, interference, and congestion. Therefore, two end devices connected to the same edge server can have different communication latencies, and the same device connected to different edge servers can also experience different communication latencies~\cite{ZHANG2019111,ZHU2024303}.
For an interaction link \(\lambda=(u,v,b)\), its raw transmission size is \(S_{\lambda}^t(h_i)\).
Let \(\psi_{\lambda}(h_i,\pi_i)\) denote the actual mechanism applied on link \(\lambda\) under mode \(h_i\) and privacy policy \(\pi_i\).
It selects the policy specified mechanism for privacy eligible links and uses \(\mathrm{none}\) for links that only transmit returned models.
\begin{equation}
\psi_{\lambda}(h_i,\pi_i)=
\begin{cases}
\pi_i(\lambda), & \lambda\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{priv}}^{h_i},\\
\mathrm{none}, & \lambda\notin\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{priv}}^{h_i}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Thus, a model return link uses \(\mathrm{none}\) even when its object type is
\(\mathrm{upd}\). The effective transmission size is
\(\widetilde{S}_{\lambda}^t(h_i,\pi_i)=
\alpha(\psi_{\lambda}(h_i,\pi_i))S_{\lambda}^t(h_i)\), where
\(\alpha(\cdot)\) is the communication inflation factor induced by the
selected privacy mechanism.
For \(\mathrm{none}\), \(\alpha(\mathrm{none})=1\).
For DP, \(\alpha(\mathrm{DP})\approx 1\). For HE, ciphertext encoding and packing increase the transmitted payload, so \(\alpha(\mathrm{HE})>1\)~\cite{PPHFL}.
In practice, \(\alpha(\mathrm{HE})\) can be obtained by offline profiling as the ratio between the serialized ciphertext payload size and the original plaintext tensor size.
The communication latency is
\begin{equation}
T_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{link},t}(h_i,\pi_i)
=
\frac{\widetilde{S}_{\lambda}^t(h_i,\pi_i)}
{R_{\lambda}^{t}}
+D_{\lambda}^{0}+T_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{priv},t}(\psi_{\lambda}(h_i,\pi_i)),
\label{eq:comm_latency}
\end{equation}
where \(R_{\lambda}^{t}=R_{u,v}^{t}\) for \(\lambda=(u,v,b)\), \(D_{\lambda}^{0}\) is the fixed base delay, and \(T_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{priv},t}\) is the link endpoint privacy processing latency. For \(\mathrm{none}\), \(T_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{priv},t}(\mathrm{none})=0\). Round level network fluctuation is represented by the time varying link rate \(R_{u,v}^{t}\).

Using this link level term, the path level link flow latency of end device \(i\), denoted by \(T_i^t(h_i,\pi_i)\), is decomposed into execution, communication, waiting, aggregation, and switching components.

The transmission indicator matrices build the communication part of the link
flow as \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{flow}}^{h_i}=
\{\lambda=(u,v,b)\mid b\in\mathcal{B},\
\mathbf{I}_{b}^{h_i}(u,v)=1\}\).
Thus, nonzero entries in \(\mathbf{I}_{b}^{h_i}\) identify the communication links induced by mode \(h_i\).
The subset \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{priv}}^{h_i}\) contains the links in \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{flow}}^{h_i}\) that require privacy protection.
Let \(N_{\lambda}(h_i)\) be the number of times link \(\lambda\) is executed in one complete link flow.
For a link executed inside a local link block, \(N_{\lambda}(h_i)=L_B\).
If the local link block is nested inside an edge aggregation loop, the count becomes \(N_{\lambda}(h_i)=L_BE_B\).
For links executed once per edge aggregation loop, \(N_{\lambda}(h_i)=E_B\).
For final aggregation upload and model return links outside repeated loops, \(N_{\lambda}(h_i)=1\).
The path level latency is decomposed as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
T_i^t(h_i,\pi_i)
&=T_i^{\mathrm{exec},t}(h_i)
+\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{flow}}^{h_i}}
N_{\lambda}(h_i)T_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{link},t}(h_i,\pi_i)\\
&\quad +T_i^{\mathrm{wait},t}(h_i)
+T_i^{\mathrm{agg},t}(h_i,\pi_i)
+T_i^{\mathrm{sw},t}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:latency_model}
\end{equation}
Here, \(T_i^{\mathrm{exec},t}\) denotes only the computation latency of the submodels executed at the end, edge, and cloud layers.
It is coupled with the selected link flow through \(h_i\), but it does not include cross layer transmission delay. That delay is accounted for separately by the summation of \(T_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{link},t}\) over \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{flow}}^{h_i}\).
\(T_i^{\mathrm{wait},t}\) denotes the waiting latency before upper layer aggregation is triggered, and \(T_i^{\mathrm{agg},t}\) denotes the aggregation latency assigned to the link flow.
Changing the collaborative mode may require execution path rescheduling and model state movement, similar to task migration and switching cost modeling in edge computing and wireless FL~\cite{ZHANG2019111,ZHU2024303,CHEN2026112204,10757319,li2026latency}.
The term \(T_i^{\mathrm{sw},t}\) denotes this mode reconfiguration latency in round \(t\), and we model it as
\begin{equation}
T_i^{\mathrm{sw},t}
=\eta_h \mathbb{I}(h_i^t\neq h_i^{t-1})
+\eta_p
\frac{\|\mathbf{p}(h_i^t)-\mathbf{p}(h_i^{t-1})\|_1}{2},
\label{eq:switching_cost}
\end{equation}
where \(\eta_h\ge 0\) is the fixed time cost incurred when the selected collaborative mode changes, similar to the unit switching cost used in dynamic FL client selection~\cite{CHEN2026112204}.
\(\eta_p\ge 0\) is the maximum variable time cost associated with model state placement change, motivated by edge service migration models where migration cost is proportional to the migrated service state size~\cite{li2026latency}.
Both coefficients are configurable or profile based system parameters.
To quantify the placement-change term in \(T_i^{\mathrm{sw},t}\), we define the normalized model state placement vector of mode \(h\) as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}(h)=
\frac{1}{Z_h}
\left[
\lvert M_L^h\rvert,
\lvert M_E^h\rvert,
\lvert M_C^h\rvert
\right],
\label{eq:model_placement_vector}
\end{equation}
where \(Z_h=\lvert M_L^h\rvert+\lvert M_E^h\rvert+\lvert M_C^h\rvert\). \(M_L^h\), \(M_E^h\), and \(M_C^h\) are the model state portions placed at the end, edge, and cloud layers under mode \(h\), respectively, and \(\lvert\cdot\rvert\) denotes profiled model state size. This normalization gives \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{p}(h)=1\), so \(\|\mathbf{p}(h_i^t)-\mathbf{p}(h_i^{t-1})\|_1/2\in[0,1]\). The normalized distance estimates the fraction of model state whose placement changes between two consecutive selected modes.

\subsubsection{Buffered Aggregation Cost}
This subsection specifies the waiting and aggregation terms used in the path latency model under buffered aggregation.
To mitigate synchronization blocking caused by slow devices, aggregation is
triggered after the required number of updates has arrived.
For edge aggregator \(j\), let \(N_j\) be the number of associated end devices and \(\rho_{\mathrm{buf}}\in(0,1]\) be the buffered aggregation participation threshold.
The number of clients needed to trigger aggregation is \(K_j=\lceil\rho_{\mathrm{buf}} N_j\rceil\).
Let \(T_i^{\mathrm{lupd}}\) be the time needed for device \(i\) to complete the collaborative update process before upload.
Let \(T_{i,j}^{\mathrm{comm},t}(h_i,\pi_i)\) denote the communication latency of the update upload from device \(i\) to edge aggregator \(j\), obtained from \(T_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{link},t}\) for the corresponding link \(\lambda=(i,j,\mathrm{upd})\).
The arrival time at edge \(j\) is
\(x_{i,j}^t=T_i^{\mathrm{lupd}}(h_i,\pi_i)+
T_{i,j}^{\mathrm{comm},t}(h_i,\pi_i)\).
Sorting arrival times as \(x_{(1),j}^t\le\cdots\le x_{(N_j),j}^t\), let
\(S_j^t(\mathbf x)=\{i_{(1)},\ldots,i_{(K_j)}\}\) be the first \(K_j\) arrived clients
selected into the buffered aggregation. The edge aggregation starts at
\(X_j^t(\mathbf x)=x_{(K_j),j}^t=\max_{i\in S_j^t(\mathbf x)}x_{i,j}^t\), and client \(i\) waits
\(W_{i,j}^{\mathrm{agg},t}=\max(0,X_j^t(\mathbf x)-x_{i,j}^t)\).
If multiple updates arrive exactly at the trigger time \(X_j^t(\mathbf x)=x_{(K_j),j}^t\), all tied updates are admitted, so \(\lvert S_j^t(\mathbf x)\rvert\) may exceed \(K_j\).
The edge aggregation time is
\(T_j^{\mathrm{agg},t}=\beta_j\sum_{i\in S_j^t(\mathbf x)}
\widetilde{S}_{i,j}^{\mathrm{upd},t}+T_j^{\mathrm{fix}}\), where \(\beta_j\)
is the aggregation processing coefficient of edge aggregator \(j\). For the
update upload link \(\lambda=(i,j,\mathrm{upd})\),
\(\widetilde{S}_{i,j}^{\mathrm{upd},t}=\widetilde{S}_{\lambda}^t(h_i,\pi_i)\),
i.e., the effective payload size of client \(i\)'s update received by edge
aggregator \(j\) after the selected privacy mechanism is applied. The
coefficient \(\beta_j\) can be obtained by offline profiling with varying
update counts and payload sizes, and \(T_j^{\mathrm{fix}}\) denotes the fixed
aggregation overhead. Privacy mechanism processing is accounted for in the
link endpoint term \(T_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{priv},t}\).

The same construction is applied to every aggregation group induced by a
complete candidate profile
\(\mathbf x=\{(h_i,\pi_i)\}_{i\in\mathcal N}\). Clients are grouped by their
selected aggregation endpoint and compatible link flow. Each edge buffer
admits its earliest required arrivals. An admitted edge aggregate that reaches
the cloud then becomes a cloud arrival, and the cloud buffer applies the same
rule to its incoming client or edge objects. Let \(A_i^t(\mathbf x)\) denote
the source clients admitted to the buffer associated with client \(i\)'s
selected path under profile \(\mathbf x\), and let
\(A^t(\mathbf x)=\bigcup_i A_i^t(\mathbf x)\) be the complete admitted client
set. The system latency \(T_{\mathrm{sys}}^t(\mathbf x)\) is the latest
completion time of the triggered terminal aggregation and model return events.
The link rates, resource state, topology, and previous profile are fixed during
one evaluation, while \(\mathbf x\) determines the path choices and arrival
times. Therefore, every candidate profile produces its own
\(A_i^t(\mathbf x)\) and \(T_{\mathrm{sys}}^t(\mathbf x)\). When
\(\rho_{\mathrm{buf}}=1\), every active buffer waits for all its members and
the same procedure reduces to full admission.

\caption{Path level link flow latency decomposition for an end device in cloud-edge-end collaborative FL.}
\label{fig:time}
\end{figure}

The directional communication terms in the figure, such as \(T_{i,j}^{\mathrm{comm},t}\), \(T_{j\rightarrow c}^{\mathrm{comm},t}\), \(T_{c\rightarrow j}^{\mathrm{comm},t}\), and \(T_{j\rightarrow i}^{\mathrm{comm},t}\), are shorthand instances of \(T_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{link},t}\) for the corresponding directed links and transmitted objects.

For this representative path, \(T_i^t(h_i,\pi_i)\) can be expanded as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
T_i^t(h_i,\pi_i)
=&\;
T_i^{\mathrm{lupd}}(h_i,\pi_i)
+T_{i,j}^{\mathrm{comm},t}(h_i,\pi_i)
+W_{i,j}^{\mathrm{agg},t}
+T_j^{\mathrm{agg},t}\\
&+T_{j\rightarrow c}^{\mathrm{comm},t}
+W_{j,c}^{\mathrm{agg},t}
+T_c^{\mathrm{agg},t}
+T_{c\rightarrow j}^{\mathrm{comm},t}
+T_{j\rightarrow i}^{\mathrm{comm},t}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:path_total_latency}
\end{equation}
The expanded form is shown only for the representative path in \figref{fig:time}. For any selected mode \(h_i\), \(T_i^t(h_i,\pi_i)\) accumulates the terms on the complete link flow induced by that mode, including the model return required to prepare the device for the next round.

\subsubsection{Privacy Budget Accounting}
The DP channel protects a model update before it crosses the trusted execution
domain. Two client datasets are adjacent when the complete contribution of one
client is replaced. A client update or an edge update computed from a client
group is clipped to norm \(C_u\). Because every clipped output lies in the
radius \(C_u\) ball, replacement sensitivity is bounded by
\(\Delta_u=2C_u\). Gaussian noise with standard deviation
\(\sigma_u\Delta_u\) is added before release. Let
\((\bar\epsilon_{i,\mathrm{upd}},\delta_{\mathrm{upd}})\) denote the fixed
privacy target for client \(i\).

For Gaussian DP, we use RDP accounting to accumulate repeated privacy
releases~\cite{mironov2017rdp}. Let
\(R_{i,\mathrm{upd}}^{t}(\alpha)\) be the accumulated RDP value of client
\(i\) at order \(\alpha>1\) before round \(t\). If candidate
\((h_i,\pi_i)\) enables DP on a cross domain update link, the new RDP increment is
\begin{equation}
\Delta R_{i,\mathrm{upd}}^{t}(\alpha;h_i,\pi_i)
=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{upd}}^{h_i}}
N_{\lambda}(h_i)\mathbb I\!\left(\pi_i(\lambda)=\mathrm{DP}\right)
\frac{\alpha}{2\sigma_u^2}.
\label{eq:privacy_budget}
\end{equation}
Here, \(\alpha\) is the RDP order, \(\sigma_u\) is the update noise
multiplier, and \(N_{\lambda}(h_i)\) is the execution count of link
\(\lambda\). Trusted internal links and HE protected links do not consume DP
budget.

Before accepting a candidate, the accumulated RDP after adding
\(\Delta R_{i,\mathrm{upd}}^{t}\) is converted to its
\((\epsilon,\delta_{\mathrm{upd}})\) value by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\epsilon_{i,\mathrm{upd}}^{t,+}(h_i,\pi_i)
&=\min_{\alpha>1}\Bigl[
R_{i,\mathrm{upd}}^{t}(\alpha)\\
&\quad+\Delta R_{i,\mathrm{upd}}^{t}(\alpha;h_i,\pi_i)
+\frac{\log(1/\delta_{\mathrm{upd}})}{\alpha-1}
\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rdp_to_dp}
\end{equation}
A candidate satisfies the privacy constraint when
\(\epsilon_{i,\mathrm{upd}}^{t,+}\le
\bar\epsilon_{i,\mathrm{upd}}\).
After the candidate is selected, the corresponding RDP increments are added to
the next round state \(R_{i,\mathrm{upd}}^{t+1}(\alpha)\).

The selector uses resource profiles, topology, client data sizes, and fixed
cost parameters declared before training. Across all rounds and cross domain
DP links, the released update transcript satisfies
\((\epsilon_{i,\mathrm{upd}},\delta_{\mathrm{upd}})\) client DP.

The total privacy target does not change with the number of training rounds.
Let \(K_{\mathrm{upd}}^{\max}(T)\) be the largest possible number of update DP
releases over \(T\) rounds. Before training, the noise multiplier \(\sigma_u\) is
chosen to satisfy
\begin{equation}
\min_{\alpha>1}\left[
\frac{K_{\mathrm{upd}}^{\max}(T)\alpha}{2\sigma_u^2}
+\frac{\log(1/\delta_{\mathrm{upd}})}{\alpha-1}
\right]\le\bar\epsilon_{i,\mathrm{upd}}.
\label{eq:noise_calibration}
\end{equation}
When \(T\) increases, the noise multiplier is recalibrated while the total
privacy target remains fixed.

\subsubsection{Estimation of Convergence Error Cost}
We next estimate the convergence error cost associated with the selected
privacy mechanisms.
For update level DP, we clip the client update difference before Gaussian perturbation~\cite{pmlr-v162-zhang22b}.
The client update vector \(u_i^t=\operatorname{vec}(\theta_i^t-w_s^t)\) is clipped and perturbed.
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    u_i^{t,\mathrm{clip}}
    &=
    \frac{u_i^t}{\max(1,\|u_i^t\|_2/C_u)},
    \\
    \tilde{u}_i^t&=u_i^{t,\mathrm{clip}}+\xi_i^t,
    &\xi_i^t&\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_u^2\Delta_u^2I_d),
    \quad \Delta_u=2C_u.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:update_dp_noise}
\end{equation}
By construction, \(\|u_i^{t,\mathrm{clip}}\|_2\le C_u\).
Under client replacement adjacency, two clipped contributions can differ by
at most \(2C_u\), which gives \(\Delta_u\).
Clipping also introduces a deterministic update distortion, which can create a persistent optimization bias when client update magnitudes are heterogeneous~\cite{pmlr-v162-zhang22b}.
To express this distortion in gradient units, we define the local clipping bias estimate as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{clip},i}^{t}
    &=
    \frac{u_i^{t,\mathrm{clip}}-u_i^t}{\eta_{\mathrm{lr}}L_B},
    \\
    \mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{clip},i}^{t}
    &=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{clip},i}^{t}\|_2^2\mid w^t\right]\\
    &=
    \mathbb{E}\!\left[
    \frac{(\|u_i^t\|_2-C_u)_+^2}{\eta_{\mathrm{lr}}^2L_B^2}
    \mathrel{\Big|} w^t\right],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:update_clipping_bias}
\end{equation}
where \((a)_+=\max(a,0)\).
The conditional mean square term \(\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{clip},i}^{t}\) is zero when the update norm does not exceed the clipping threshold almost surely and increases with the amount removed by clipping.
For normalized aggregation weights \(a_i\), Jensen's inequality gives
\(\mathbb{E}[\|\sum_i a_i\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{clip},i}^{t}\|_2^2\mid w^t]
\le\sum_i a_i\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{clip},i}^{t}\), so the weighted sum of local terms is a separable upper bound on the aggregate clipping bias.
Update clipping and Gaussian perturbation affect convergence in different ways.
The clipping distortion is generally nonzero in expectation and is therefore
treated as a deterministic gradient error. Its conditional mean square
\(\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{clip},i}^{t}\) contributes to the persistent bias term
in the PL error bound.

In contrast, Gaussian update noise has zero mean and contributes through its
variance. Its contribution depends on the aggregation weight used by the
actual training process. Let \(A_C^t(\mathbf x)\) contain the clients
whose updates are admitted and reach the cloud under profile \(\mathbf x\).
For edge \(e\), let \(N_e\) denote its total data size and let
\(N_{e,A}^t\) denote the admitted cloud data size. The normalized weight of
client \(i\) is
\begin{equation}
q_i^t(\mathbf x)=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle
\frac{N_{e(i)}}{\sum_{e:N_{e,A}^t>0}N_e}
\frac{n_i}{N_{e(i),A}^t},
& i\in A_C^t(\mathbf x),\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:effective_aggregation_weight}
\end{equation}
These weights are the edge normalized weights used by the cloud aggregation
step. They satisfy \(\sum_i q_i^t(\mathbf x)=1\). Independent client noise
therefore contributes
\begin{equation}
\mathbb E\left\|\sum_i q_i^t(\mathbf x)\xi_i^t\right\|_2^2
=\sum_i\left(q_i^t(\mathbf x)\right)^2\sigma_u^2\Delta_u^2d.
\label{eq:aggregated_update_variance}
\end{equation}
For LIEIIIC and LIIEIIIC, Update DP may also be applied after edge
aggregation. Let \(\mathcal G^t(\mathbf x)\) be the resulting edge update
groups and let \(q_g^t=\sum_{i\in g}q_i^t\). The variance of an independent
edge update noise is weighted by \((q_g^t)^2\). The complete derivation is
provided in Appendix~\ref{app:convergence_derivation}.

We assume that the original global objective \(F(w)\) is \(G\)-smooth and satisfies the \(\mu\)-PL condition
\(\|\nabla F(w)\|_2^2\ge 2\mu(F(w)-F(w^*))\).
Combining local stochastic variance, update clipping bias, and update DP
variance gives a one step recursion
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[F(w^{t+1})-F(w^*)]
\le
(1-\rho_{\mathrm{PL}})\mathbb{E}[F(w^t)-F(w^*)]
+\mathcal{R}(h_i,\pi_i).
\label{eq:pl_recursion}
\end{equation}
Taking the steady state of this recursion motivates the convergence error cost
used during selection. Let \(m_{u,i}^t\) be the number of update DP releases
generated by the selected link flow. Define the client bias and variance costs as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
B_i^t={}&\frac{3m_{u,i}^t}{2\mu}
\mathcal B_{\mathrm{clip},i}^t,\\
V_i^t={}&\frac{G\eta_{\mathrm{lr}}L_B}{\mu}\left[
\sigma_{\mathrm{local}}^2
+m_{u,i}^t\frac{\sigma_u^2\Delta_u^2d}
{\eta_{\mathrm{lr}}^2L_B^2}\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:client_error_cost}
\end{equation}
For an edge update group \(g\), \(B_g^t\) and \(V_g^t\) are defined by the
update clipping and noise terms in \equref{eq:client_error_cost} for the DP
release applied after edge aggregation. These terms are zero when that release
uses HE. These client and edge terms are combined below with the cloud fusion
penalty.

\subsection{Global Multi-Objective Collaborative Optimization}
\label{sec:global_optimization}
The previous subsection defines the resource feasibility, path level latency,
privacy budget, and convergence error cost terms for one device.
Cloud-edge-end FL ultimately trains a system level global model.
If each device independently selects the locally fastest and lowest estimated
cost mode, cross edge information fusion may be insufficient, especially under
non-IID data.
Let \(\mathbf{h}_{\mathcal{N}}=[h_1,\dots,h_N]\) and \(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathcal{N}}=[\pi_1,\dots,\pi_N]\) denote the global mode and privacy policy vectors.

Path level latency \(T_i^t\) describes the time contribution generated by the
path selected for device \(i\) at decision epoch \(t\). A complete profile
changes the path choices, arrival order, buffer triggers, and model return
events. For \(\mathbf x=(\mathbf h_{\mathcal N},\boldsymbol\pi_{\mathcal N})\),
the buffer procedure above produces \(A_i^t(\mathbf x)\) for every client and
the corresponding system latency \(T_{\mathrm{sys}}^t(\mathbf x)\).
Slower updates excluded by their corresponding buffers do not block the
current event. Every candidate profile is evaluated with its own admitted
sets.

The cloud fusion ratio is
\begin{equation}
r_C^t(\mathbf x)
=
\frac{\sum_{i\in A_C^t(\mathbf x)}n_i}{N_{\mathrm{total}}},
\quad
N_{\mathrm{total}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}n_i.
\end{equation}
It measures the sample proportion participating in cloud level cross edge
fusion. To penalize insufficient cloud fusion, define
\(\Psi_C^t(\mathbf x)=
\xi/[r_C^t(\mathbf x)+\varepsilon]\), where \(\xi\) controls
penalty strength and \(\varepsilon\) avoids division by zero.
The system level estimate of convergence error cost is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathrm{sys}}^t(\mathbf x)
={}&\sum_{i\in A_C^t(\mathbf x)}
\left[q_i^t B_i^t+(q_i^t)^2V_i^t\right]\\
&+\sum_{g\in\mathcal G^t(\mathbf x)}
\left[q_g^tB_g^t+(q_g^t)^2V_g^t\right]
+\Psi_C^t(\mathbf x).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:global_omega}
\end{equation}
The future training loss is unavailable during selection, so this quantity
estimates its error cost. Equation~\equref{eq:global_omega} uses the same
admitted clients and aggregation weights as the training process. When many
devices remain in edge only modes, \(r_C^t\) decreases and the cloud fusion
penalty increases.

Let
\(\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{h}_{\mathcal N},\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathcal N})\)
denote a complete system decision. Its global feasible set is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal X^t=\bigl\{\mathbf{x}\mid{}&h_i\in\mathcal H_i^{\mathrm{res}},\
\pi_i\in\Pi(h_i),\\
&\epsilon_{i,\mathrm{upd}}^{t,+}(h_i,\pi_i)
\le\bar\epsilon_{i,\mathrm{upd}},\ \forall i\in\mathcal N\bigr\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:global_feasible_set}
\end{equation}
The global multi objective problem minimizes system latency and the system
level estimate of convergence error cost.
\begin{equation}
\underset{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal X^t}{\operatorname{minimize}}\quad
\mathbf f^t(\mathbf{x})=
\begin{bmatrix}
T_{\mathrm{sys}}^t(\mathbf{x})\\
\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathrm{sys}}^t(\mathbf{x})
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:global_multiobjective}
\end{equation}
The Pareto optimal decision set and its objective space Pareto front are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal X_{\mathrm P}^t
&=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal X^t\mid\nexists\mathbf{x}'\in\mathcal X^t,
\ \mathbf{x}'\prec\mathbf{x}\},
\\
\mathcal F_{\mathrm P}^t
&=\{\mathbf f^t(\mathbf{x})\mid\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal X_{\mathrm P}^t\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pareto_front}
\end{equation}
Here \(\prec\) denotes Pareto dominance. The latency range is
\([T_{\min}^t,T_{\max}^t]\), and the estimated cost range is
\([\widehat\Omega_{\min}^t,\widehat\Omega_{\max}^t]\), both evaluated over
\(\mathcal X_{\mathrm P}^t\). The normalized objectives are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\bar T^t(\mathbf{x})&=
\frac{T_{\mathrm{sys}}^t(\mathbf{x})-T_{\min}^t}
{T_{\max}^t-T_{\min}^t+\varepsilon_n},\\
\bar\Omega^t(\mathbf{x})&=
\frac{\widehat\Omega_{\mathrm{sys}}^t(\mathbf{x})
-\widehat\Omega_{\min}^t}
{\widehat\Omega_{\max}^t-\widehat\Omega_{\min}^t+\varepsilon_n}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pareto_normalization}
\end{equation}
Here \(\varepsilon_n>0\) prevents division by zero. The reference point
\(\mathbf z^{r,t}=[z_T^{r,t},z_\Omega^{r,t}]^\top\) can be prescribed or set
to the computed ideal point
\([T_{\min}^t,\widehat\Omega_{\min}^t]^\top\). Its normalized form is
\(\bar{\mathbf z}^{r,t}=[\bar z_T^{r,t},\bar z_\Omega^{r,t}]^\top\).
Using the normalized Tchebycheff distance to the reference or ideal
point~\cite{steuer1983tchebycheff}, we define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
D_\infty^t(\mathbf{x})
&=\max\!\left\{
\left|\bar T^t(\mathbf{x})-\bar z_T^{r,t}\right|,
\left|\bar\Omega^t(\mathbf{x})-\bar z_\Omega^{r,t}\right|
\right\},\\
\mathbf{x}^\star
&\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal X_{\mathrm P}^t}
D_\infty^t(\mathbf{x}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:selection}
\end{equation}
Thus, the selected profile minimizes its largest normalized deviation from
the reference or ideal point. If several profiles attain the same minimum
distance, the solver returns one minimizer.
Real test accuracy is used in experiments to evaluate the effect of the
estimated convergence error cost objective.

\section{Algorithm}
\label{sec:algorithm}

Each device first constructs \(\mathcal S_i\) by retaining the pairs
\((h_i,\pi_i)\) that satisfy the resource and privacy constraints. A complete global profile selects one
pair from every \(\mathcal S_i\) and is evaluated by
\((T_{\mathrm{sys}}^t,\widehat\Omega_{\mathrm{sys}}^t)\).

For a small instance, the exact solver evaluates all
\(M=\prod_{i\in\mathcal N}|\mathcal S_i|\) global profiles. Here,
\(|\mathcal S_i|\) is the number of feasible candidates for device \(i\), and
the product over all devices gives the total number of joint profiles. Sorting the
resulting pairs by increasing latency and scanning the estimated cost gives
the exact Pareto set. Applying \equref{eq:selection} then gives one
ideal point Tchebycheff solution over the exact Pareto set. If one global evaluation costs
\(C_{\mathrm{eval}}\), this procedure costs
\(O(MC_{\mathrm{eval}}+M\log M)\). Its exponential value of \(M\) limits it
to small scale validation.

For full scale selection, a bounded search initializes a profile archive and
expands each retained profile by replacing one device decision at a time. A
deterministic exploration queue containing at most \(K_{\mathrm B}\) evaluated
intermediate profiles allows the search to pass through a temporarily
dominated profile. The method maintains a bounded archive containing at most
\(K_{\mathrm P}\) profiles that
are non dominated among the evaluated candidates. Here,
\(K_{\mathrm P}\ge 2\) is the prescribed maximum archive size and controls
the tradeoff between Pareto front coverage and search complexity. Because only a subset of
\(\mathcal X^t\) is explored, the resulting archive
\(\widehat{\mathcal X}_{\mathrm P}^t\) approximates the exact Pareto set
\(\mathcal X_{\mathrm P}^t\). The exact and approximate solvers use the same
objective evaluation, normalization, and ideal point Tchebycheff selection.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:selection} presents the bounded solver.

To describe the search cost, let
\(K_{\mathrm{cand}}=\max_i|\mathcal S_i|\) be the largest number of
candidates available to one device. From one retained profile, changing one
device at a time generates at most \(N(K_{\mathrm{cand}}-1)\) neighboring
profiles. Since the archive and exploration queue contain at most
\(K_{\mathrm P}+K_{\mathrm B}\) profiles, one expansion evaluates at most
\(L\le (K_{\mathrm P}+K_{\mathrm B})N(K_{\mathrm{cand}}-1)\) neighboring profiles. Evaluating
them costs \(O(LC_{\mathrm{eval}})\), and sorting them for Pareto filtering
costs \(O(L\log L)\). Therefore, at most \(I_{\max}\) expansions cost
\(O(I_{\max}L(C_{\mathrm{eval}}+\log L))\). Direct evaluation of the two
system objectives aggregates the terms of \(N\) devices, so
\(C_{\mathrm{eval}}=O(N)\) in the worst case. The implementation caches each
candidate flow and updates the weighted error terms and affected buffer groups
incrementally for a one device replacement.

The search is performed only at the strategy update points determined by
\(S_P\). At other rounds, the previous profile is reused. The latency caused
by changing collaborative modes is included in \(T_i^t\).

The implementation applies one explicit utility stability safeguard before
the bounded search. The update Gaussian standard deviation relative to its
clipping radius is
\begin{equation}
q_{\mathrm{upd}}=2\sigma_{\mathrm{upd}}.
\label{eq:cloud_dp_stability}
\end{equation}
The factor two follows from the replacement sensitivity \(\Delta_u=2C_u\).
If a cloud reaching DP candidate has
\(q_{\mathrm{upd}}>\tau_{\mathrm{DP}}\), it is removed only when the same client has a
feasible cloud reaching HE candidate. We use
\(\tau_{\mathrm{DP}}=1\). A cloud reaching DP candidate with a per coordinate
Gaussian standard deviation larger than the update clipping scale is
replaced by an HE alternative when such an alternative is feasible.

Because the reported task is global model training, the feasible global set
also excludes configurations with too little cross edge information. For every
represented edge domain \(j\), define
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal H_C=\{&\mathrm{LIIC},\mathrm{LIEIIC},\\
&\mathrm{LIEIIIC},\mathrm{LIIEIIIC}\}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The executed profile satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{\sum_{i:e(i)=j}n_i\mathbb I(h_i\in\mathcal H_C)}
{\sum_{i:e(i)=j}n_i}
\ge r_{C,\min}.
\label{eq:edge_cloud_coverage}
\end{equation}
We use \(r_{C,\min}=0.5\) in the main experiments and expose it as an
experimental parameter. Initial profiles are moved into this feasible set by
deterministic one device replacements. Every subsequent archive and exploration
profile is required to satisfy the same condition. The final Tchebycheff
selection is therefore made directly over the constrained feasible set.

The coverage lower bound alone does not make the admitted cloud sample mass
identical across edge domains. Direct sample count FedAvg would therefore
overweight an edge whenever its temporary cloud reaching fraction is larger,
which is particularly harmful under edge level non-IID data. We remove this
participation rate bias without observing labels. Let
\(N_j=\sum_{i:e(i)=j}n_i\) be the full data mass associated with edge \(j\),
\(A_{j,C}^{t}(\mathbf x)=\{i\in A^t(\mathbf x)\mid e(i)=j,
h_i\in\mathcal H_C\}\), and
\(N_{j,C}^{t}(\mathbf x)=\sum_{i\in A_{j,C}^{t}(\mathbf x)}n_i\). The cloud applies
edge normalized FedAvg,
\begin{equation}
a_i^t(\mathbf x)=\frac{N_{e(i)}}{N_{e(i),C}^{t}(\mathbf x)}n_i,
\qquad
\Delta w^t(\mathbf x)=
\frac{\sum_j\sum_{i\in A_{j,C}^{t}(\mathbf x)}a_i^t(\mathbf x)\Delta w_i^t}
{\sum_j\sum_{i\in A_{j,C}^{t}(\mathbf x)}a_i^t(\mathbf x)}.
\label{eq:edge_normalized_fedavg}
\end{equation}
Thus, clients admitted from the same edge retain sample proportional weights,
whereas the total weight of a represented edge equals its full data mass
\(N_j\), independent of its round specific cloud reaching fraction. For an
edge aggregated object, the same expression is applied to its aggregate sample
count. For an HE protected update, the public scalar \(a_i^t(\mathbf x)\) is applied
before CKKS encryption. The evaluator adds the resulting ciphertexts and any
unprotected public terms, while the trusted coordinator decrypts only the
final weighted sum. Under \equref{eq:edge_cloud_coverage}, every represented edge has
\(N_{j,C}^{t}(\mathbf x)>0\).

\begin{breakablealgorithm}
\caption{Bounded Pareto Solver for Collaboration Reconfiguration}
\label{alg:selection}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Device set \(\mathcal N\), resource feasible modes \(\mathcal H_i^{\mathrm{res}}\), policy sets \(\Pi(h_i)\), update RDP states \(R_{i,\mathrm{upd}}^t(\alpha)\), targets \((\bar\epsilon_{i,\mathrm{upd}},\delta_{\mathrm{upd}})\), round system state, path latency and buffer models, \(K_{\mathrm P},K_{\mathrm B},I_{\max},S_P\), optional \(\mathbf z^{r,t}\), and \(\mathbf x^{t-1}\)
\ENSURE Selected global profile \(\mathbf x^\star=\{(h_i^\star,\pi_i^\star)\}_{i\in\mathcal N}\)
\STATE \textbf{Preparation}
\STATE For every \(i\in\mathcal N\), construct the candidate set satisfying the resource and privacy constraints
\STATE \(\displaystyle
\mathcal S_i=\{(h_i,\pi_i)\mid h_i\in\mathcal H_i^{\mathrm{res}},
\pi_i\in\Pi(h_i),
\epsilon_{i,\mathrm{upd}}^{t,+}(h_i,\pi_i)
\le\bar\epsilon_{i,\mathrm{upd}}\}\).
\STATE If client \(i\) has a feasible cloud reaching HE candidate, remove each
cloud reaching DP candidate with \(q_{\mathrm{upd}}>\tau_{\mathrm{DP}}\) from
\(\mathcal S_i\) according to \equref{eq:cloud_dp_stability}.
\STATE \(\displaystyle
\mathcal X^t=\left\{\mathbf x\in\prod_{i\in\mathcal N}\mathcal S_i
\mid \mathbf x\text{ satisfies \equref{eq:edge_cloud_coverage}}\right\},\quad
\mathbf x=(\mathbf h_{\mathcal N},\boldsymbol\pi_{\mathcal N})\).
\IF{\(t\) is not a strategy update point and \(\mathbf x^{t-1}\in\mathcal X^t\)}
    \RETURN \(\mathbf x^{t-1}\).
\ENDIF
\begin{samepage}
\STATE \textbf{Objective Evaluation}
\STATE For every generated profile \(\mathbf x\), sort its path arrivals at
each edge and cloud buffer and obtain \(A_i^t(\mathbf x)\) and
\(T_{\mathrm{sys}}^t(\mathbf x)\).
\end{samepage}
\STATE Compute \(q_i^t(\mathbf x)\), \(r_C^t(\mathbf x)\), and
\(\widehat\Omega_{\mathrm{sys}}^t(\mathbf x)\) from the admitted cloud
updates using \equref{eq:effective_aggregation_weight} and
\equref{eq:global_omega}.
\STATE \textbf{Approximate Pareto Search}
\STATE Construct \(\mathbf x_T\), \(\mathbf x_\Omega\), and three deterministic intermediate profiles from normalized local objective weights.
\STATE Move any infeasible initial profile into \(\mathcal X^t\) by deterministic one device replacements.
\STATE Retain the non dominated initial profiles as \(\mathcal A_0\) and set \(\mathcal B_0=\emptyset\).
\FOR{\(r=1,\ldots,I_{\max}\)}
    \STATE Generate all unvisited feasible profiles \(\mathcal V_r\subseteq\mathcal X^t\) that differ from a profile in \(\mathcal A_{r-1}\cup\mathcal B_{r-1}\) at one device.
    \STATE Evaluate \((T_{\mathrm{sys}}^t,\widehat\Omega_{\mathrm{sys}}^t)\) for every \(\mathbf x\in\mathcal V_r\).
    \STATE \(\displaystyle
    \mathcal A_r=\bigl\{\mathbf x\in\mathcal A_{r-1}\cup\mathcal V_r\mid
    \nexists\mathbf x'\in\mathcal A_{r-1}\cup\mathcal V_r,\ 
    \mathbf x'\prec\mathbf x\bigr\}\).
    \IF{\(|\mathcal A_r|>K_{\mathrm P}\)}
        \STATE Retain both objective endpoints and \(K_{\mathrm P}-2\) approximately uniform profiles in latency order.
    \ENDIF
    \STATE Retain at most \(K_{\mathrm B}\) newly evaluated profiles outside \(\mathcal A_r\) with the smallest normalized ideal point distance as \(\mathcal B_r\).
    \IF{\(\mathcal A_r=\mathcal A_{r-1}\) and \(\mathcal B_r=\emptyset\)}
        \STATE \textbf{break}
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\STATE Set \(\widehat{\mathcal X}_{\mathrm P}^t\leftarrow\mathcal A_r\).
\STATE \textbf{Ideal Point Tchebycheff Selection}
\STATE Compute \(T_{\min}^t,T_{\max}^t,
\widehat\Omega_{\min}^t,\widehat\Omega_{\max}^t\) over
\(\widehat{\mathcal X}_{\mathrm P}^t\).
\STATE \(\displaystyle
\bar T^t(\mathbf x)=
\frac{T_{\mathrm{sys}}^t(\mathbf x)-T_{\min}^t}
{T_{\max}^t-T_{\min}^t+\varepsilon_n},\quad
\bar\Omega^t(\mathbf x)=
\frac{\widehat\Omega_{\mathrm{sys}}^t(\mathbf x)-
\widehat\Omega_{\min}^t}
{\widehat\Omega_{\max}^t-\widehat\Omega_{\min}^t+\varepsilon_n}\).
\STATE Use the prescribed \(\mathbf z^{r,t}\), or set it to
\([T_{\min}^t,\widehat\Omega_{\min}^t]^\top\), and apply the same normalization to obtain
\((\bar z_T^{r,t},\bar z_\Omega^{r,t})\).
\STATE \(\displaystyle
D_\infty^t(\mathbf x)=\max\!\left\{
|\bar T^t(\mathbf x)-\bar z_T^{r,t}|,
|\bar\Omega^t(\mathbf x)-\bar z_\Omega^{r,t}|
\right\}\).
\STATE \(\displaystyle
\mathbf x^\star\in
\arg\min_{\mathbf x\in\widehat{\mathcal X}_{\mathrm P}^t}
D_\infty^t(\mathbf x)\).
\RETURN \(\mathbf x^\star\).
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\(1.47\times10^{-9}\). These measurements determine the HE communication and
computation coefficients used in the main experiments.

\subsection{Compared Methods and Main Results}
Ours performs joint Pareto search over the global client profile and selects a

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presented a dynamic cloud-edge-end collaboration reconfiguration
framework for federated learning under resource and privacy constraints. The
unified collaboration space represents execution placement, aggregation
topology, transmitted objects, return paths, and privacy mechanisms as one
global decision. The joint model evaluates latency, communication, privacy
consumption, encrypted aggregation overhead, and estimated convergence error
cost. The constrained multiobjective formulation is solved by bounded Pareto
search and ideal point Tchebycheff selection.

The current framework uses profiled computation and link rates in its logical
latency model, while the training process and CKKS aggregation are executed on
one host. Future work will combine online profiling with physical
cloud-edge-end deployment and will further reduce reconfiguration overhead
through parallel profile evaluation, distributed search, and warm starts.

\section{Derivation of the Convergence Error Cost Estimate}
\label{app:convergence_derivation}

This appendix provides the intermediate steps used to estimate convergence
error cost in Section~\ref{sec:cost_utility_model}. It separates the effects
of update clipping and Gaussian update noise.

\subsection{PL Error Recursion}

Let the deterministic parameter gradient perturbation caused by update
clipping be
\begin{equation}
e_i^t=e_{\mathrm{uclip},i}^t.
\label{eq:app_total_bias}
\end{equation}
For consistency with the separable upper bound used during selection, we use
the conservative bound
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[\|e_i^t\|_2^2\mid w^t]
\le3\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{upd}}^{i,t}
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{clip},i}^t.
\label{eq:app_combined_bias_bound}
\end{equation}
To make the coefficients in \equref{eq:client_error_cost} explicit, define the
effective round step size
\begin{equation}
\alpha=\eta_{\mathrm{lr}}L_B
\label{eq:app_effective_step}
\end{equation}
and write one perturbed update in gradient units as
\begin{equation}
w^{t+1}=w^t-\alpha
\left[\nabla F(w^t)+e_i^t+s_i^t\right],
\label{eq:app_perturbed_update}
\end{equation}
where \(e_i^t\) is the deterministic bias in \equref{eq:app_total_bias} and
\(s_i^t\) is conditionally zero mean stochastic error. Let
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
V_i^t
&=\mathbb{E}[\|s_i^t\|_2^2\mid w^t]\\
&\le \sigma_{\mathrm{local}}^2
+m_{u,i}^t\frac{\sigma_u^2\Delta_u^2d}{\alpha^2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:app_total_variance}
\end{equation}
Dividing the update perturbation by \(\alpha\) converts it to gradient units,
which explains the factor \(\alpha^{-2}\) in
\equref{eq:app_total_variance}. After admission, the client terms are
combined using \(q_i^t(\mathbf x)\). The independent variance terms are
therefore multiplied by \((q_i^t)^2\), as shown in
\equref{eq:aggregated_update_variance}. Noise added after edge aggregation is
combined using the corresponding group weight \(q_g^t\).

Let \(g^t=\nabla F(w^t)\), and condition on \(w^t\). By \(G\)-smoothness and
\(\mathbb{E}[s_i^t\mid w^t]=0\),
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_t[F(w^{t+1})]
&\le F(w^t)-\alpha\langle g^t,g^t+e_i^t\rangle\\
&\quad+\frac{G\alpha^2}{2}
\left(\|g^t+e_i^t\|_2^2+V_i^t\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:app_smooth_descent}
\end{equation}
Using the exact identity
\begin{equation}
\langle g,g+e\rangle
=\frac{1}{2}\|g\|_2^2+\frac{1}{2}\|g+e\|_2^2
-\frac{1}{2}\|e\|_2^2,
\label{eq:app_inner_product_identity}
\end{equation}
and assuming \(0<\alpha\le1/G\), \equref{eq:app_smooth_descent} becomes
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_t[F(w^{t+1})]
&\le F(w^t)-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|g^t\|_2^2
-\frac{\alpha}{2}(1-G\alpha)\|g^t+e_i^t\|_2^2\\
&\quad+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|e_i^t\|_2^2
+\frac{G\alpha^2}{2}V_i^t\\
&\le F(w^t)-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|g^t\|_2^2
+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|e_i^t\|_2^2
+\frac{G\alpha^2}{2}V_i^t.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:app_descent_bias_variance}
\end{equation}
The PL inequality implies
\(-\alpha\|g^t\|_2^2/2\le
-\mu\alpha[F(w^t)-F(w^*)]\). Define
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\mathrm{PL}}=\mu\alpha=\mu\eta_{\mathrm{lr}}L_B.
\label{eq:app_pl_factor}
\end{equation}
Substituting \equref{eq:app_combined_bias_bound} into
\equref{eq:app_descent_bias_variance} therefore gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_t[F(w^{t+1})-F(w^*)]
&\le(1-\rho_{\mathrm{PL}})[F(w^t)-F(w^*)]\\
&\quad+\frac{3\alpha}{2}S_{\mathrm{bias},i}^t
+\frac{G\alpha^2}{2}V_i^t,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:app_pl_recursion}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{bias},i}^t
=\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{upd}}^{i,t}\mathcal B_{\mathrm{clip},i}^t.
\label{eq:app_bias_sum}
\end{equation}
Because \(G\alpha^2V_i^t/2\le G\alpha^2V_i^t\), we use the conservative residual
\begin{equation}
\mathcal R(h_i,\pi_i)
=\frac{3\alpha}{2}S_{\mathrm{bias},i}^t+G\alpha^2V_i^t.
\label{eq:app_residual_definition}
\end{equation}

For a fixed conditional residual, iterating the recursion gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}[F(w^t)-F(w^*)]
&\le(1-\rho_{\mathrm{PL}})^t[F(w^0)-F(w^*)]\\
&\quad+\frac{1-(1-\rho_{\mathrm{PL}})^t}{\rho_{\mathrm{PL}}}
\mathcal R(h_i,\pi_i).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:app_recursion_unrolled}
\end{equation}
Thus its persistent contribution is estimated by
\(\mathcal R(h_i,\pi_i)/\rho_{\mathrm{PL}}\). Substituting
\(\alpha=\eta_{\mathrm{lr}}L_B\), \equref{eq:app_total_variance}, and
\equref{eq:app_bias_sum} gives the client terms \(B_i^t\) and \(V_i^t\) in
\equref{eq:client_error_cost}. Update terms are multiplied by their numbers of
DP releases. The same calculation gives
\(B_g^t\) and \(V_g^t\) for a DP release applied after edge aggregation.
Weighting the bias terms by \(q_i^t\) or \(q_g^t\), and independent variance
terms by their squared weights, gives \equref{eq:global_omega}.

\section{CKKS Implementation and Security Scope}
\label{app:he_model}
The HE path uses the CKKS approximate arithmetic scheme~\cite{cheon2017ckks}.
Each policy run generates one key pair. Clients and edge nodes use the public
key to encrypt every parameter of an HE protected update. The cloud evaluator
does not hold the secret key and performs only ciphertext addition. A trusted
coordinator holds the secret key and decrypts the final aggregate. The
implementation uses polynomial modulus degree 8192, coefficient modulus bits
\([50,40]\), scale \(2^{40}\), and chunks of at most 4096 values. The
same key pair is reused within one policy run.

This protection hides selected update contents from the communication links
and the cloud evaluator. It does not hide the final aggregate from the trusted
coordinator and does not address malicious updates or collusion with the
coordinator. The implementation provides a complete SEAL execution path for
these roles. The validation experiment records ciphertext count, encrypted
parameter count, operation time, and maximum absolute error against plaintext
aggregation. The main utility experiments use the measured
\(\alpha_{\mathrm{HE}}\) and \(T^P(\mathrm{HE})\) values to represent CKKS
communication and computation costs without repeating the same cryptographic
benchmark in every training round.
